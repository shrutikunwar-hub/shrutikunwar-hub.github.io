01 / Problem Statement

The Question

Static pricing leaves money on the table. Whether it's a grocery store deciding between three price points for an item, or a concert venue dynamically adjusting ticket prices as the show date approaches — the question is the same: how does a pricing agent learn the optimal price from experience, without knowing demand in advance?

This project tackled two distinct but related challenges. First, a Multi-Armed Bandit experiment testing three pricing arms (ε-Greedy, with and without contextual signals like weather). Second, a Reinforcement Learning model where ticket prices adapt dynamically based on how many days remain before a concert — learning from willingness-to-pay data across 10,000 simulated visitors.

02 / Methodology

The Approach

Two experiments, three algorithms, and a key question running through both: does adding information (context, state) meaningfully improve pricing decisions?

01

MAB Setup

T=1000 rounds, K=3 price arms ($1.59, $1.79, $1.99), ε=0.1, N=5. Profit data read from ObserveProfit_contextual.xlsx.

02

Contextual MAB

Same setup, but the algorithm also reads ObserveContext.xlsx — market context including a binary rainy-day signal that shifts optimal pricing.

03

RL Q-Learning

S=5 states (days before concert), C=10,000 visitors, 3 price arms ($40, $60, $80), δ=0.99 discount factor. Learns a Q-table via Bellman updates.

03 / Algorithms

How the Agents Decide

Three exploration-exploitation strategies were implemented and compared. Each balances uncertainty differently.

Core Algorithm
ε-Greedy
Exploits the best known arm 90% of the time, explores randomly the other 10%. Simple, interpretable, and the backbone of both MAB and contextual MAB experiments.
ε = 0.1 · Explore rate: 10%
Optimistic
UCB
Upper Confidence Bound adds an exploration bonus inversely proportional to how often an arm has been tried. Naturally explores under-tested arms without a fixed epsilon.
Confidence bonus · No tuning needed
Bayesian
Thompson Sampling
Maintains a probability distribution over expected rewards and samples from it. Adapts exploration based on uncertainty — tends to outperform in practice with limited data.
Beta posterior · Probability matching

06 / Key Findings

What the Models Revealed

Context Unlocks a Hidden Pricing Strategy

+13.1% Profit Gain

The standard MAB agent converged on $1.59 for 91.4% of rounds — an apparently safe choice. But it was leaving money on the table during high-demand conditions. The contextual agent discovered that $1.99 is optimal when the market signal is rainy, shifting arm selection dramatically and generating $84.03 in additional profit across 1,000 rounds.

Perishability Changes Everything Near the Deadline

State-Dependent Pricing

The RL model confirmed that pricing for a perishable good isn't static — it's a function of remaining time. With 3+ days to go, $60 is optimal because the option value of future selling opportunities justifies holding out. With 1–2 days remaining, the cost of not selling outweighs the margin benefit, so the model drops to $40 to maximize conversion probability.

Discount Factor Encodes Urgency

δ = 0.99

Even with a discount factor close to 1 (meaning the model values future rewards nearly as much as immediate ones), the Bellman update still creates urgency as the deadline approaches — because fewer future periods remain. The model "knows" that a dollar earned in state 5 is worth slightly less than one earned in state 1, reinforcing the price-drop pattern near showtime.

Exploration Strategy Matters Less Than Information

Algorithm ≠ Edge

Across both experiments, the biggest performance difference didn't come from switching algorithms (ε-Greedy vs Thompson vs UCB) — it came from adding contextual information. A simple ε-Greedy agent with context outperformed a non-contextual agent regardless of how sophisticated its exploration strategy was. Information quality dominates algorithmic sophistication.

07 / Business Implications

What to Do About It

These findings translate directly into actionable pricing principles for real-world applications — from retail and SaaS to events and hospitality.

R.01

Invest in Contextual Signals First

Before optimizing the exploration algorithm, identify what market signals (day of week, weather, demand proxies, inventory level) might shift the optimal price. The contextual MAB result shows a 13% lift from a single binary signal — real-world context can be far richer.

R.02

Price Perishables Dynamically Near Expiry

Hotels, airlines, event venues, and food retailers should all implement state-dependent pricing that automatically discounts as the perishability deadline approaches. A static markdown schedule is better than nothing — but a learned Q-table adapts to actual demand patterns.

R.03

Start with ε-Greedy; Upgrade Thoughtfully

Thompson Sampling and UCB require more infrastructure and calibration. For a new pricing system, ε-Greedy provides a solid, interpretable baseline. Upgrade to Bayesian methods once demand distributions are better understood and the business can validate model uncertainty estimates.

R.04

Run Experiments Continuously

The exploration rate (ε=0.1) ensures the model never fully stops learning. In production, this translates to always keeping a small percentage of traffic on non-greedy prices — protecting against demand shifts that would otherwise leave a static policy stranded on an outdated optimum.
